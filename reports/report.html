Beyond the Box Score

Most one-year leaps by young NBA players are regression to the mean. Once you account for that, the box score's real growth signal is offensive load and touch, not shooting efficiency.

01The question

Rookie-scale extensions, trades for young players and minutes on a rebuilding roster are all bets on development. The question is always the same: is this player going to get better?

The obvious approach is to study last year's most-improved players and look for what they had in common. That mostly rediscovers luck. So I asked a harder version: once a disciplined projection already accounts for age and regression to the mean, what else predicts growth?

The sample is every player aged 24 or younger with at least 500 minutes since 2000-01. The outcome is next-season Box Plus/Minus (BPM), a box-score estimate of points per 100 possessions above average. Each season was forecast using only information that existed at the time.

02Most “improvement” is luck reversing

If one-year changes were pure development, the average change would be flat across starting points. It isn't. Players coming off a season of −4 BPM or worse gained about two points the next year, however many minutes they played. Players at +4 or better over 1,500+ minutes held steady. The same level in fewer minutes gave back almost two points.

Of the ten biggest BPM jumps from 2024-25 to 2025-26, 9 started from a negative season (average −2.3). Some, like Stephon Castle, are probably real growth. A raw leaderboard can't tell which.

Development is also front-loaded: about +1.2 BPM a year at 19-20, falling to +0.6 at 22 and +0.3 at 23-24. Survivors flatter that curve. Players projected in the bottom fifth fall out of the rotation 20% of the time, against 3% in the top fifth, so every result here was re-checked with inverse-probability weighting.

03The bar to clear

A model has to beat a disciplined projection, not a strawman. Marcel, adapted from Tom Tango's baseball system, weights the last three seasons 5/4/3 by minutes. It blends them with 250 minutes of league-average prior and adds a fitted age adjustment. It already knows about age and regression to the mean.

Each season from 2016-17 to 2025-26 was predicted by models refit only on earlier seasons. That gives 1,258 out-of-sample player-seasons, exactly what a front office could have done in real time. Hyperparameters were tuned on pre-2016 data only, and confidence intervals resample players, not rows.

04Bet on volume and touch, discount hot shooting

Adding feature groups one at a time showed where the edge comes from. Per-possession box-score rates were the only group that clearly helped (−0.029 RMSE, CI −0.049 to −0.011). On/off plus-minus, shot location, draft slot, height and team quality added nothing distinguishable from zero once the box score was known.

To see which rates matter, I regressed the gap between actual BPM and Marcel's projection on standardized season signals, with errors clustered by player.

What a front office could do with it

  • Discount efficiency-driven breakouts. A young role player whose BPM rests on above-league true shooting at low usage is likelier to regress than one with the same BPM built on creation.
  • Read free-throw percentage as a development signal, not just a free-throw signal.
  • Don't over-penalize turnovers in young players carrying a big offensive load.

The effects are modest: 0.1 to 0.2 BPM per standard deviation. Together they explain 5.7% of what Marcel misses. This sharpens priors and points film study; it doesn't replace scouting.

The common thread is load. Stephon Castle (25% usage, free-throw rate +0.21 over league) and Keyonte George (28% usage, 88% shrunk FT%) carry big roles at modest efficiency, the profile the signals favor.

The counterexample. Kon Knueppel posted +2.8 BPM as a rookie. Marcel, which sees only BPM and age, projects +3.5. The model projects +2.8, because his season leaned on above-league true shooting (+5 points) with a below-average free-throw rate at 22% usage.

That isn't a call that he's overrated. It's a flag that his profile resembles shooting-driven seasons that tend not to build further, and the first thing I'd check on film.

The gaps (about 1 to 1.7 BPM) are smaller than the ±2.2 BPM intervals. Treat them as scouting leads, not verdicts. This forecast will be scored against 2026-27 results.

Where it fails

  • Role changes and injuries. The biggest upside misses were breakouts tied to new roles: Victor Oladipo after the 2017 trade to Indiana, Giannis Antetokounmpo in 2016-17, Ja Morant in 2021-22. The biggest downside misses were mostly injury-shortened seasons. The box score can't see either coming.
  • Intervals undercover when minutes collapse: 69% coverage for players who end up with 250–999 minutes, against 85% and 82% in the higher-minute bins.
  • BPM's blind spots carry through. It under-rates defense that doesn't produce steals, blocks or rebounds, and it isn't RAPM or tracking-based impact.
  • No market context. Nothing here speaks to salary or contract value.

What would make it better

  • A multi-year RAPM target, so the model isn't only predicting a box-score formula.
  • Tracking data (drives, touches, matchups) and injury and role data for the largest misses.
  • A separate minutes model: “will he improve?” and “will he play?” are different questions.
Method: Python (pandas, scikit-learn, statsmodels). Rolling-origin validation, player-clustered bootstrap, online split-conformal intervals, empirical-Bayes shrinkage of FT% and 3P%. The whole analysis regenerates from one command, python -m bbs.pipeline, with 44 automated tests. Code: github.com/doxxk/nba-player-analytics.
